Case Study

Cornell Enrollment Mobile App

A mobile first redesign of Cornell University Student Center focused on making add, drop, and swap feel clear and safe under time pressure.

Interaction design Mobile Prototype

Overview

The Cornell Enrollment Mobile App is a mobile first redesign of Cornell University Student Center. It transforms a complex enrollment portal into a guided smartphone experience built for clarity and confidence. The redesign focuses on reducing confusion and uncertainty during high stakes actions such as adding, dropping, and swapping classes.

1. Empathize

Enrollment is time sensitive and emotionally high stakes. Students are often making decisions quickly during add and drop week, sometimes on their phone between classes. I focused on where the current experience creates hesitation, anxiety, or avoidable mistakes, and what students need to feel confident pressing confirm.

What I looked for

  • Where stress spikes. Moments where students pause, back out, or re-check because they are not sure what happens next.
  • Mobile constraints. One-hand use, quick scanning, and interrupted usage while walking or multitasking.
  • Risk perception. Why drop and swap feel irreversible, dangerous, or confusing even when the user knows what they want.
  • Decision-making signals. The minimum info students need to choose quickly: status, conflict, impact, and confirmation.

Informal interviews and observations

I ran quick informal interviews and walkthroughs with Cornell students where they showed how they currently check schedules, hunt for course info, and attempt add, drop, or swap. I paid attention to what they clicked first, what they ignored, and where they had to slow down to avoid mistakes.

  • Confidence matters as much as information. Students want to know what will happen before submitting. Confirmation is as important as navigation.
  • Students want direct access to key actions. They do not want to hunt through menus to reach schedule, search, add, drop, and swap.
  • Students want clarity on rules and consequences. Waitlist behavior, time conflicts, unit limits, and what swap actually does should be explained in plain language.
  • Status is the first thing they scan. Open, Waitlist, and Closed need to be visible immediately without reading a dense table.

Research synthesis

I translated raw notes into grouped themes to guide design decisions. This helped me prioritize speed, trust during risky actions, reduced cognitive load, and better failure-state handling.

2. Define

People problem: During add and drop week, students need to make high stakes academic decisions quickly, often while moving between buildings. The current enrollment experience is confusing and not mobile friendly, which increases stress and the risk of mistakes.

Core problems to solve

  • Navigation complexity. Students jump between multiple pages and dense menus. The flow is hard to follow when rushed.
  • Lack of trust during risky actions. Drop and swap are not explained clearly. Students do not feel reassured that the action is safe or reversible.
  • Cluttered information display. Long tables make scanning difficult. Status such as Open, Waitlist, and Closed is not visually prominent enough to support fast decisions.
  • Weak feedback loops. After submitting, students still feel unsure whether the change actually processed correctly.

Target audience: Cornell undergraduates ages 18 to 25 who manage enrollment during busy academic periods and want fast decisions, clear feedback, and mobile convenience without needing a laptop.

Goal: Create a mobile first enrollment experience that makes add, drop, and swap feel clear, guided, and safe by reducing clutter, simplifying navigation, and providing strong feedback and confirmations.

3. Ideate

Based on the problems above, I explored solution directions that reduce stress and cognitive load while respecting enrollment constraints. The goal was to make the interface feel predictable: students should always know what step they are on, what will happen next, and what changed after submitting.

Design directions

  • Simplify layout. Replace table-heavy pages with card-based UI and highlight only the information students need to decide quickly.
  • Make actions guided. Break add, drop, and swap into structured steps: select, review, confirm, result.
  • Build trust. Add warnings before risky actions and confirmation screens that clearly state what changed with one clear next step.
  • Create a clear navigation system. Use bottom navigation for key tasks: Home, Search, Schedule, Enrollment Hub, Profile.

What I prioritized from research

  • Mobile-first speed. Reduce taps, reduce reading, reduce page-hunting.
  • Status visibility. Make Open, Waitlist, Closed scannable where decisions happen.
  • Edge cases and recovery. Support time conflicts, full classes, and “what do I do now” moments.
  • Psychological safety. Make risky actions feel reversible and clearly explained before the user commits.

4. Prototype

I created an interactive mobile prototype that reflects a realistic enrollment journey, from checking today’s schedule to safely confirming schedule changes. I started with mid-fidelity wireframes to lock the structure and flow logic, then moved into high-fidelity screens for clarity and polish.

Testing and iteration

I ran quick usability checks with students using realistic tasks: add a class, verify it appears on the schedule, drop safely with a warning, and complete a swap without confusion. The goal was not just completion, but confidence.

  • Participants: 6 Cornell undergraduates across different years.
  • Tasks: Add from search, confirm enrollment, check schedule, drop with warning, swap course A to course B.
  • What improved: Faster scanning for status, fewer “back” clicks, and more confidence after success screens.
  • What I refined: Clearer swap comparison, stronger primary CTA on success screens, and consistent placement for status indicators.

This redesign does not only modernize the interface. It improves how students feel while using the system by reducing uncertainty, increasing confidence during risky actions, and enabling faster decision-making in time-sensitive enrollment moments.
